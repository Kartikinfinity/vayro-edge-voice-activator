Executive Technical Summary

What was built. A two-part voice activation system. An ESP32-S3 microcontroller with an INMP441 digital microphone listens all the time. Every 10 ms it turns the last 30 ms of sound into 40 log-mel numbers, and every 60 ms a 10 665-parameter int8 depthwise-separable CNN (DS-CNN) scores the last 1.02 s of those numbers for the custom keyword "Hey Vayro". When the mean of three consecutive scores reaches 0.80, the device starts streaming audio — beginning 571 ms before the detection, so the keyword's tail and anything said straight after it are included — over a TCP connection that was opened at boot. A Python server on a PC decides when the speaker has finished (energy-based voice-activity detection), and transcribes the command with the open-source Vosk (Kaldi) recogniser. Nothing leaves the device until a detection happens. VERIFIED

Final configuration. Product firmware (firmware/sdkconfig.product: decisions DEC-22 and DEC-24), model v2b at threshold 0.80 (SHA-256 f7c913efdbb28f64…, decision DEC-26), ESP-IDF 5.5.5, esp-tflite-micro 1.4.1 with esp-nn 1.4.1, and the threaded ASR server with Vosk en-in-0.5. VERIFIED

The engineering that mattered most. Six results shaped the final system and are explained in Chapters 10–13:

  1. The microphone data was captured one bit late with the textbook I2S framing; a measured alignment sweep switched the firmware to MSB-justified framing (E-18).
  2. Device features differed from training features in 3 of 5 998 frames because the chip's maths library rounds differently from the PC's; the host's tables are now copied into the device, making them bit-identical (E-21, DEC-16).
  3. The first "product" build used 260.2 KiB — over the limit — once instruction code in internal RAM was counted; configuration trims brought the streaming peak to 245.0 KiB (E-42, E-52, E-53, E-60).
  4. The server's speech recogniser blocked its own network reads, which backed audio up into the device and caused 3 of 31 streams to overrun; moving recognition to its own thread fixed it (E-58, E-60).
  5. A fourth model (v4) passed its pre-declared selection rule but failed badly on the first new speaker (14/25 vs 20/25), so the team shipped v2b in a documented override (E-59, DEC-26).
  6. The final latency test on the exact shipped configuration gave 138 ms, replacing an earlier 95 ms figure measured on a different build without the recogniser running (E-37, E-63).

Main limitations. False triggers with TV or family speech (15.8 per hour) are the largest open problem. Only two real speakers were tested. The busiest core's idle margin is about half a percentage point, and while streaming it reaches 10.5 %. Part of the latency (the "capture term") is estimated, not measured, and no logic analyser was available. Power was never measured. The server address is compiled into the firmware, and the firmware waits up to 30 s for a USB host at boot. The ASR's word accuracy is that of an off-the-shelf model (publisher WER 36 %). This is a working, physically tested prototype, not a production-ready product (Chapter 14).

Chapter 1 Project Overview and Engineering Objective

1.1 The project in one paragraph

Think of a smart speaker, but split in two. A small, cheap chip sits in the room and does only one job: it waits to hear one particular phrase, "Hey Vayro". It never sends the room's sound anywhere while it waits. When it hears the phrase, it immediately starts sending the audio — including a little of what came just before, so nothing is cut off — to a more powerful computer that works out what the person asked for ("turn on the light"). The chip is an ESP32-S3 microcontroller with a digital INMP441 microphone; the computer is a PC running an open-source speech recogniser. The project's job was to make the chip's part small, fast, accurate and provably within the limits the problem statement sets. VERIFIED

1.2 The problem being addressed

The problem statement (PS 26172, from ISRO) describes a common situation in voice-controlled devices. If a device sends all sound to a cloud recogniser, three things go wrong: it costs a lot (the server processes silence and background noise all day), it invades privacy (the room is always being recorded somewhere else), and it is slow (every request waits for the network). The PS asks for a hybrid: the edge device handles the "wake-up" moment locally, and the cloud does the heavy recognition only after that. VERIFIED PS text

Technically, the PS asks for three things (full list in Chapter 3):

  1. A keyword spotting (KWS) model — a small neural network that recognises one phrase — running locally on a low-power device.
  2. On detection, streaming the audio that follows to a remote automatic speech recognition (ASR) server, with minimal data overhead and latency.
  3. Doing this under hard limits: less than 256 KB of RAM and less than 10 % CPU while idle-listening, open-source tools only, no vendor wake-word SDK, and a keyword that the team trained itself.
In simple words

"Keyword spotting" and "speech recognition" are different jobs. Keyword spotting answers one yes/no question over and over ("was that 'Hey Vayro'?"). Speech recognition turns any speech into words. The first is small enough for a microcontroller; the second is not. This project runs the first on the chip and the second on a PC.

1.3 Real-world use and intended users

The PS gives the setting only as "voice-controlled IoT" under the theme Smart Automation; it names no user group or deployment environment. The project did not define a specific deployment either. Everything tested ran in one home: a desk, a household with a television and family members, and a home Wi-Fi network. Application ideas that appear in the team's documents (laboratory control rooms, assistive use, home automation) are PLANNED ideas, not tested use cases. 1.5 Expected system behaviour

In words, the system is meant to behave like this: while nobody says the keyword, the device listens, computes, and sends nothing but occasional small clock-synchronisation packets. When someone says "Hey Vayro" and then a command, the device detects the keyword within a fraction of a second, and the server receives audio that starts shortly before the detection and continues until the speaker stops, then prints what was said. A second keyword said within two seconds of the first is ignored (the "refractory" period), and only one stream is open at a time.

1.7 Operating sequence

  1. The board is powered from the PC's USB port. The firmware starts, waits for the PC's capture tool to open the port (up to 30 s), prints its identity and memory state, and checks the microphone.
  2. It builds the feature extractor and the neural network, joins Wi-Fi, and opens one TCP connection to the server at a fixed address. It keeps that connection alive with a clock-synchronisation ping every 10 s.
  3. From then on, core 1 processes audio in 10 ms steps without stopping; core 0 handles the network.
  4. When the averaged score reaches 0.80, the device queues a stream request and carries on listening.
  5. The streaming task sends a START message, then the 571 ms of stored audio as fast as the link allows, then new audio every 40 ms.
  6. The server watches the level of the incoming audio. After 0.7 s of quiet (or 1.5 s with no speech at all) it tells the device to stop. The device sends END with its own statistics.
  7. The server finishes recognition in a separate thread and prints and stores the transcript. The device is already listening for the next keyword.

1.8 What was actually achieved

By 30 September 2026 the complete chain existed on real hardware and had been measured (Table 0.1 in the Executive Summary). The final firmware fits the strict RAM reading with about 5 kB to spare, idles below 10 % CPU on each core, detects the keyword with 88 % recall on a held-out session of the training speaker and 80 % on a new speaker, triggers falsely about once in five quiet hours but about 16 times an hour when a television is on, and delivers audio to the server a median 138 ms after the keyword ends. Chapters 10 to 14 give the evidence and the limits of each of these statements.

2.3 The study's method

The study is disciplined about the difference between known and estimated. 2.4 Key technical principles in the study

This section explains the study's main ideas in plain language, because the whole implementation follows from them.

2.4.1 The microphone sets hard rules

  • The INMP441 only speaks one I2S dialect. It needs exactly 64 bit-clock pulses per stereo frame, so every sample travels in a 32-bit slot holding a 24-bit value. At 16 kHz the bit clock is 64 × 16 000 = 1.024 MHz. There is no 16-bit mode, so the firmware must convert. (P-02, P-03)
  • Its low-pass filter scales with the sample rate. The flat pass band ends at 0.423 × fs, which is 6 768 Hz at 16 kHz. Standard keyword front ends use frequencies up to ~7.5 kHz, so the top bands would see a microphone roll-off that training audio does not have. The study's fix (option O1) is to stop the mel filterbank at 6 500 Hz in both training and firmware. (P-16, §2.3b)
  • It has a fixed delay of 17.2 samples (1.078 ms at 16 kHz), and outputs zeros for 2^18 clock cycles after power-up (≈ 256 ms), so the design includes a 300 ms MIC_SETTLE state. (P-15, P-19)

2.4.2 Memory: the model is not the problem

The study's memory budget is its most important finding. Estimated peak use was about 227 KiB without encryption. The model and its working memory were only 17 % of that; the Wi-Fi driver, TCP/IP stack and operating system were 58 %. Its advice: "optimisation effort should follow the bytes, not the intuition". It also argued that PSRAM must be disabled — using the board's extra 8 MB of external RAM would make the 256 KB limit meaningless — and that a TLS handshake at trigger time would break both the memory and the latency budget. 2.4.3 CPU: use the vector unit and never ship reference kernels

The study estimated the whole always-on pipeline at ≈ 2.8 % of one core at 240 MHz, with the FFT (the step that turns sound into frequencies) as the biggest single cost, and warned that forgetting the optimised kernels (esp-dsp for FFT, esp-nn for the network) would multiply the cost by 5–20×. It proposed measuring CPU three ways, with the MLPerf Tiny GPIO duty-cycle method as the primary instrument. DOCUMENTED §4

2.4.4 The model: shortlist, and train from scratch

After mapping 12 model families, the study concluded that CPU is not the discriminator on this chip — every candidate fits. What matters is whether TensorFlow Lite Micro has kernels for every operation, whether there is any false-activation evidence, and how much streaming state the model needs. It shortlisted a microWakeWord-style reference, a TC-ResNet8-style primary, and a dilated-convolution "false-activation hardened" model, to be compared on detection-error curves. It insisted on training from random initialisation so the PS's ban on pre-trained assistant keywords could never be argued. DOCUMENTED §5, §6.4

2.4.5 Compliance: two traps

  • ESP-SR / WakeNet: Espressif's own speech SDK is one line of configuration away on this chip, is a vendor wake-word SDK with a "Custom" licence, ships binary models, and includes "Alexa" as a wake word — so it breaks three PS rules at once. The study required an automated build gate against it (failure mode X25). DOCUMENTED §6.3
  • The Wi-Fi blob: ESP-IDF is open source, but its Wi-Fi/PHY libraries are closed binaries. The study's position: this is a radio driver, not a voice-activation SDK, it is outside the KWS pipeline, and it must be disclosed in a software bill of materials rather than hidden. DOCUMENTED §6.5

2.4.6 Buffering: derive the pre-roll, share one ring

The device only fires after the keyword ends, so it must keep recent audio. The study derived the minimum stored audio as detection lag (≤ 374 ms) + 250 ms lead-in + 100 ms scheduling jitter = 724 ms, rounded to a 1.0 s ring (32 000 bytes), with pre-computed smaller rows for shorter smoothing windows (for a 5-score window: 571 ms in a 0.75 s ring). It also proposed a single ring buffer with several read positions instead of three copies, saving about 34 KB. DOCUMENTED §8.3, §8.4

2.4.7 Transport: open the connection at boot

Opening a TCP connection (and certainly a TLS session) when the keyword fires puts a network round trip on the measured path. The study chose plain TCP opened at boot and kept warm, 20 ms PCM frames, an 8-byte header, and an OVERRUN marker so that a stream the device could not keep up with ends short rather than corrupted. DOCUMENTED §8.5, §9

2.4.8 Latency: three definitions, because the PS is ambiguous

"The time delta between the keyword ending and the cloud ASR receiving the audio stream" can mean detection → server (L1), acoustic end of the keyword → first audio at the server (L2, the literal reading), or keyword end → the server holding audio up to real time (L3). The study instrumented all three and estimated L1 ≈ 13 ms, L2 ≈ 205 ms. DOCUMENTED §10

2.4.9 Decision logic and the refractory period

The study proposed averaging the last 5 scores with a 0.95 threshold, and — critically — a 2 s refractory period: without it, one spoken keyword whose score stays high for 200 ms would produce six detections, and any false-activation count would be meaningless (X14). DOCUMENTED §8.6

2.4.10 The parity rule

A single JSON file defines the front end; the firmware compiles it, the training code reads it, and a "golden vector" test must show the device and the PC produce bit-identical features from the same audio. The file's hash is embedded so that a model trained with a different front end refuses to load. DOCUMENTED §7.3

2.4.11 The recommended architecture

The study's decision was "Architecture A as the built spine, with a measurement-gated promotion path to Architecture B": build the shared skeleton (capture, front end, ring, transport, server, instrumentation) once, start with a small 1-D DS-CNN, and swap only the model and decision parameters if measurements justified it. Architecture C (speculative streaming, UDP) was not to be built. DOCUMENTED "Architecture decision"

3.3 Measurable constraints

3.3.1 RAM: < 256 KB

Why it exists. To force a genuinely small edge footprint. The ambiguity. The PS does not say what counts (the whole device? the application? the model?) or whether KB means 1 000 or 1 024 bytes. Design response. The project uses the strictest reading, S4: everything in the chip's internal RAM that the application occupies at its peak — global variables, instruction code copied into RAM (IRAM), every task stack, the Wi-Fi and TCP/IP buffers, the tensor arena, the audio ring — with PSRAM disabled so nothing can hide in external memory, and compares it with both 256 000 B and 262 144 B.

How it is measured. S4 = static internal RAM from the linker (idf.py size: .bss + .text in IRAM + .data + vectors) plus the peak heap use, computed as the heap's total size minus its lowest free value reported during a run that includes streaming. The 96 KB of on-chip SRAM configured as instruction and data cache is excluded as hardware-reserved (a stated choice, see Chapter 12). Result: 250 899 B — passes 256 000 B by 5 101 B and 262 144 B by 11 245 B. Met VERIFIED (E-60).

3.3.2 CPU: < 10 % while idling in continuous listening

Why it exists. An always-on device must leave most of the processor free. The ambiguity. "10 % CPU" on a dual-core chip can mean the busiest core or both cores averaged; "idle" here means "listening but no keyword", not "doing nothing". Design response. All always-on work is on core 1; networking is on core 0; both scopes are reported. How measured. FreeRTOS run-time statistics every 10 s (@CPU), cross-checked with cycle counters around the front end and inference (@PERF). Result: busiest core 9.32 % mean, 9.51 % max; both cores 5.53 % (E-60); a 12-minute run gave core-1 max 9.04 % (E-56). Met, with thin margin on the per-core reading VERIFIED. While streaming (not idle) core 1 reached 10.5 % — outside the requirement but reported (Chapter 12).

3.6 Communication requirements

The PS asks for "minimal data overhead" without defining it. The design choices and their consequences:

  • Nothing is transmitted while idle except one clock-synchronisation exchange every 10 s: PING (8-byte header + 8 B), PONG (8 + 24 B) and INFO (8 + 16 B) — 72 bytes of application data, plus TCP/IP headers. VERIFIED netstream.c

  • Protocol overhead: each audio packet carries 1 280 B of audio and an 8-byte header — 0.6 % at the application layer. With a 40-byte TCP/IP header per packet (one segment per packet, since the configured TCP MSS is 1 440 B and TCP_NODELAY is set), the overhead on the network is about 3.7 %. This figure is calculated, not measured with a packet capture. INFERRED
  • Per activation: a stream of 2.08 s (a keyword with no command, as in T1) carries 66 560 B of audio; a keyword + command of ~3.5 s about 112 kB. INFERRED from measured stream lengths

4.5 The interfaces, explained

4.5.1 I2S (the microphone link)

I2S is a three-wire serial audio bus. The bit clock (BCK/SCK) ticks once per data bit. The word select (WS) line changes level at the start of each channel's slot and so runs at the sample rate. The data line (SD) carries the sample bits, most significant bit first. The ESP32-S3 is the master: it generates both clocks, and the microphone clocks its data out on them.

The only subtlety that mattered is when the first bit appears. In "Philips" framing the MSB arrives one bit clock after WS changes; in "MSB-justified" framing it arrives on the WS edge. Pick the wrong one and every sample is shifted by one bit: the sound is still recognisable (only a factor of two and a lost bit), which is exactly why this kind of fault is dangerous — it looks almost right. The M1 alignment sweep tested four combinations and picked the one where the data behaved like real audio (Chapter 11).

4.5.2 USB-Serial-JTAG (the evidence link)

The S3's native USB port appears on the PC as a serial port. The project uses it as a framed, CRC-checked binary link (serial_link.c): every message — text telemetry, raw audio, features, model outputs — is wrapped in a frame with a 4-byte sync word, type, length and a CRC-16. This made it possible to record hours of audio through the real microphone and to compare the device's internal numbers with the PC's, which is where most of the project's evidence comes from.

4.5.3 Wi-Fi and TCP (the product link)

The board joins the home network as a Wi-Fi station and keeps one TCP connection to the server. Chapter 6 describes the protocol.

4.5.4 GPIO (instrumentation)

Two pins are toggled by the firmware purely for measurement, following the MLPerf Tiny method the study recommended: GPIO16 is high while a 10 ms hop is being processed (its duty cycle is the pipeline's CPU share) and GPIO15 pulses on each detection. Without a logic analyser they were never read. VERIFIED code; measurement NOT RUN

4.6 Clocking and data-rate arithmetic

  • Sample rate 16 000 Hz × 64 bit clocks per frame = 1.024 MHz bit clock.
  • Per 10 ms hop: 160 frames. In mono mode each frame is one 32-bit word, so the DMA delivers 640 bytes per hop (64 000 B/s); in stereo it would be 1 280 bytes (128 000 B/s).
  • The firmware keeps only 16 bits per sample: 320 bytes per hop, 32 000 B/s — the same rate that goes over Wi-Fi during a stream.
  • DMA buffering: 4 descriptors × 160 frames = 40 ms of audio, so the processing task can be late by up to ~30 ms without losing samples. The driver counts overflows (i2s_ovf), and every product run reported 0 (e.g. E-60). VERIFIED

4.7 Power

No current or energy measurement was made. Two design choices increase power on purpose: the CPU runs at 240 MHz (the study showed that halving the clock would roughly double the CPU percentage, and CPU is a hard gate while power has no target), and Wi-Fi modem sleep is disabled (WIFI_PS_NONE) so that packets from the server are not delayed to beacon intervals. The microphone's own 2.2 mA is negligible. Any statement about the device's power consumption would currently be UNVERIFIED.

Chapter 6 Complete System Architecture

6.1 The big picture

The system has three subsystems connected by two links:

  1. The edge device (ESP32-S3 firmware): captures audio, computes features, runs the keyword model, decides, keeps a short audio history, and streams on demand.
  2. The network: one Wi-Fi association and one TCP connection on the home LAN.
  3. The ASR server (Python on a PC): receives streams, timestamps them, ends them when the speaker stops, recognises the command, and records everything.

A fourth element, the host capture tool on the same PC, reads the device's USB telemetry. It is not needed for the system to work, but almost all the evidence in this report was collected through it.

6.4 Data flow

Follow one piece of sound through the system:

  1. Microphone → DMA. The INMP441 shifts out a 24-bit sample every 62.5 µs. I2S0's DMA collects 160 of them (10 ms) into one of four buffers.
  2. DMA → int16. reader_task wakes when a buffer is full, takes the 160 32-bit words and keeps bits 31:16 (the top 16 bits of the 24-bit sample).
  3. int16 → ring. The 160 samples are appended to the 0.75 s ring. Nothing reads them there unless a detection happens.
  4. int16 → features. The same 160 samples go to the front end, which slides its 30 ms window by 10 ms and outputs 40 numbers. They are converted to int8 and stored as row f mod 100 of a 100-row feature array.
  5. features → score. On every sixth frame the 100 rows are copied, oldest first, into the model's input tensor (through a 256-entry lookup table that maps the front end's int8 scale onto the model's), and the model is invoked. Its int8 output becomes a probability p.
  6. score → decision. The mean of the last three p values is compared with 0.80, and a trigger is allowed only if at least 2 000 ms have passed since the last one.
  7. decision → stream. A trigger puts a small record (frame number, ring write position, detection time, raw score) into the streamer's one-slot queue — without waiting. If the streamer is already busy or the network is down, the trigger is logged with streamed=0.
  8. ring → network. The streamer reads the ring from 571 ms before the recorded write position and sends it in 1 280-byte packets; then it keeps up with new audio.
  9. network → server. The server timestamps every packet, feeds the audio to its voice-activity detector and to the ASR thread, and tells the device to stop when the speaker has finished.

6.5 Control flow, initialisation and runtime behaviour

Two design points in the boot sequence deserve attention:

  • The firmware waits for the PC before starting audio (link_wait_host(30000)), because on the native USB port anything printed before the PC opens the port is lost, and the M1 boot diagnostics were the evidence. If no PC opens the port, the wait times out after 30 s and boot continues. For a stand-alone device this is a 30-second delay after every power-up. VERIFIED code
  • The M1 microphone probes still run at every boot — a 1.3 s stereo probe, four 0.3 s alignment captures, and one or two 0.55 s mono probes — and they decide which slot mask to use. They cost about 3 s of boot time and make each boot print a fresh microphone health check. VERIFIED code

6.6 Communication between device and server

The protocol is binary and minimal. The architecture study's header had no length field, so each message type has a fixed payload size, and both sides use a lookup table (PAYLOAD in ingest.py). The receiver resynchronises on the magic byte if it ever loses track. VERIFIED

6.9 Operational sequence (final system)

  1. Power and boot. USB power; the firmware waits for the capture tool (≤ 30 s), prints @BOOT (IDF version, firmware hash, chip, PSRAM off), runs the microphone probes (≈ 3 s), initialises the front end with canonical tables and the model (@KWS … run=v2b … arena_used=13404), zeroes the ring, starts Wi-Fi.
  2. Connect. Wi-Fi gets an address (@NET wifi=up), the TCP connection opens (@NET tcp=connected), eight quick pings establish the clock offset.
  3. Listen. Core 1 processes 100 hops per second; the model runs 16.7 times per second; the main task prints @STATS/@PERF every second and @CPU every ten.
  4. Detect. The averaged score crosses 0.80: GPIO15 pulses, @KW is printed, the streamer is woken.
  5. Stream. START, then the 571 ms backlog in about 14 packets, then live 40 ms packets.
  6. End. The server's VAD decides the command is over and sends END; the device replies with END and its statistics (@STREAM … reason=1 … min_slack_ms=179).
  7. Recognise. The server's ASR thread finishes decoding, prints the command and writes <stem>.wav, <stem>_asr.wav, <stem>.json and a row in activations.csv.
  8. Continue. Listening never paused; the next keyword can be detected two seconds after the previous one.

7.2 Build entry points

The firmware is always built through tools/idf.ps1, never with a bare idf.py. The wrapper does four things before calling ESP-IDF:

  1. puts Python 3.12 first on the PATH and sources IDF's export.ps1;
  2. runs tools/forbidden_deps.py — the build stops on any hit;
  3. runs tools/gen_frontend_config.py — regenerates ps_frontend_config.h from frontend.json only if it changed (keeping the file's timestamp stable avoids needless rebuilds);
  4. assembles the SDKCONFIG_DEFAULTS list and deletes the build's sdkconfig if any defaults file is newer, because ESP-IDF would otherwise keep the old values silently.

Build variants live in separate folders: build_usbjtag (capture/demo build), build_usbjtag_product (the final product build, -ExtraDefaults firmware\sdkconfig.product -BuildTag product), build_usbjtag_nonet (no Wi-Fi), build_uart.

The model enters the firmware through tools/gen_model_source.py <artifact folder>: it checks that metrics.json describes exactly that .tflite (SHA-256), writes the bytes as a 16-byte-aligned const array (so they stay in flash), writes the model's SHA-256 and the front-end SHA-256 its training data used, computes the 256-entry input lookup table exactly as the evaluation code does, and writes sdkconfig.model from the matching selection outcome.

7.3 Firmware, in execution order

7.3.1 app_main() — boot (firmware/main/app_main.c)

app_main runs once on core 0. In order:

  1. link_init() installs the USB-Serial-JTAG driver and redirects every ESP_LOG call into framed TEXT messages, so logs can never corrupt binary audio frames.
  2. link_wait_host(30000) blocks until the PC sends a byte (the capture tool sends S when it opens the port) or 30 s pass.
  3. inst_boot_report() prints @BOOT (IDF version, first 9 hex digits of the firmware ELF hash, build date, cores, chip revision, PSRAM flag, flash size, CPU MHz, reset reason); @CFG prints the pins, framing and DMA layout; inst_mem_report("boot") prints heap totals.
  4. Microphone probes. probe() opens I2S in a given slot mode, discards a settling period, then accumulates per-slot statistics (slot_stats_t): mean (DC), RMS, peak, the fraction of words with any of the low 8 bits set (lowbyte_nz, which must be ~0 because the INMP441 drives only 24 bits), the fraction whose 24-bit LSB is 1 (lsb1, ~50 % when correctly aligned), zeros, and the first non-zero sample after the clock started. The stereo probe runs first so it sees the first clock after boot; alignment_sweep() repeats it for Philips/MSB × normal/inverted bit clock and prints @ALIGN; the mono probe (EXP-10) then confirms which slot mask carries the microphone.
  5. ps_fe_init() builds the front end from the constants generated from frontend.json; then, only if ps_fe_tables_fe_sha256 equals the firmware's PS_FE_JSON_SHA256, ps_fe_set_tables() overwrites the filterbank weights and PCAN lookup table with the host twin's canonical values, and @FETAB prints eight table hashes for comparison with the PC.
  6. kws_init() loads the model (next section) and @KWS prints its identity: run=v2b model_sha256=f7c913efdbb28f64 model_bytes=28024 arena_bytes=16384 arena_used=13404 win=100 bands=40 stride=6 avg_n=3 thr_pct=80 refractory_ms=2000.
  7. ring_init() zeroes the ring; ns_start() starts NVS, Wi-Fi and the two network tasks on core 0.
  8. xTaskCreatePinnedToCore(reader_task, "i2s_reader", CONFIG_PS_READER_STACK, …, 20, …, 1) starts the audio pipeline on core 1 at priority 20.
  9. The main loop wakes every second: prints @STATS (samples, drop counters, I2S overflows, level in dBFS, heap free and heap minimum) and @PERF (stack high-water marks, front-end and inference cycle counts, trigger count); every tenth second calls cpu_report(); and polls the USB link for a T byte, which starts a stream exactly like a detection but marked synthetic=1 — the hook that made memory and transport tests possible without speaking (EXP-1).

7.3.2 reader_task() — the always-on pipeline

This is the most important function in the firmware. Its loop body, lightly abridged:

if (ac_read(raw, sizeof raw, &got, 1000) != ESP_OK || got != sizeof raw) { s_short_reads++; continue; }
gpio_set_level(CONFIG_PS_INST_ACTIVE_GPIO, 1);          /* I-1 duty-cycle pin: start of this hop's work */
for (int i = 0; i < HOP; i++) i16[i] = (int16_t)(raw[i] >> 16);   /* bits 23:8 of the 24-bit sample */
ring_write(i16, HOP);                                    /* pre-roll source for the streamer */
const uint32_t c0 = esp_cpu_get_cycle_count();
while (off < HOP) {                                      /* at most one feature frame per hop */
    const int produced = ps_fe_process(i16 + off, HOP - off, &used, feat);

}
const uint32_t dc = esp_cpu_get_cycle_count() - c0;     /* front-end cycles (I-2) */

Problem it solves: do all periodic work in one place so its cost is exactly the "idle listening" CPU. Inputs: 160 × int32 words per call. Outputs: ring contents, one feature frame, possibly a trigger. Why this design: the hop is the natural clock; one task on one core cannot be pre-empted by Wi-Fi; the cycle counter brackets give per-stage attribution. Tested by: feature parity (E-21), posterior parity (E-22), live-equals-replay (E-26), and the CPU runs (E-43, E-56, E-60).

The >> 16 deserves a note. The microphone's 24-bit sample sits in bits 31:8 of the 32-bit I2S word; shifting right by 16 keeps bits 31:16, i.e. the top 16 bits of the sample. The host capture tool derives its int16 files as int24 >> 8, which is the same value, so device and PC always see identical 16-bit audio. The measured room floor was about 52 units RMS in this representation, so quantisation noise is about 45 dB below it and the truncation costs nothing measurable (DEC-09).

Three details make the device's behaviour reproducible on the PC:

  • The 100-row feature array s_fring is a circular buffer of int8 rows; kws_set_row writes each row straight into the model's input tensor through the lookup table (the "zero-copy" change from EXP-1 that removed a separate 4 KB window buffer).
  • The three-score average starts from zeros, exactly like np.convolve(p, ones(3)/3, "full") in eval_stream.py, so the first detections after boot match the offline replay (E-26: 27 of 27 trigger times identical).
  • t_ms = 30 + 10·f — the time of the last sample in frame f's window — is printed with every @KW so detections can be located in recorded audio.

7.3.4 audio_capture.c — I2S

ac_open() creates an I2S0 receive channel in master role with dma_desc_num = 4 and dma_frame_num = 160, a standard-mode slot configuration for 32-bit data (MSB or Philips template from sdkconfig.board), the three GPIOs, no MCLK, and registers on_recv_q_ovf to count DMA overflows. ac_read() is a thin wrapper around i2s_channel_read(). Keeping this component small made it easy to reopen the channel with different framings during the M1 sweep.

7.3.5 audio_ring.c — the unified ring buffer

static int16_t s_buf[RING_SAMPLES];            /* CONFIG_PS_RING_MS * 16 samples = 12 000 in the product */
static _Atomic uint32_t s_w;                   /* monotonic write index (wraps at 2^32) */

void ring_write(const int16_t *x, size_t n) {
    uint32_t w = atomic_load_explicit(&s_w, memory_order_relaxed);
    for (size_t i = 0; i < n; i++) s_buf[(w + i) % RING_SAMPLES] = x[i];
    atomic_store_explicit(&s_w, w + (uint32_t)n, memory_order_release);   /* publish after the data */
}
int ring_read(uint32_t idx, int16_t *dst, size_t n) {
    /* 0 if not written yet, or if already overwritten (lapped); copy; re-check after copying */
}

The indices are sample counts since boot, not array positions, so "571 ms before the trigger" is simply w − 9136, and "has the writer overtaken me?" is w − idx > RING_SAMPLES. The release/acquire atomics make the one-writer (core 1), one-reader (core 0) pattern safe without a mutex on the audio path, and the reader checks again after copying because the writer may lap it mid-copy. It implements the architecture's "retention is a property of not overwriting yet" idea (§8.4). The front end does not need a ring cursor: it consumes the fresh hop directly.

7.3.6 microfrontend/ — features

ps_frontend.c is a 99-line wrapper around the vendored TensorFlow Lite Micro "microfrontend". ps_fe_init() fills a FrontendConfig with every parameter from frontend.json — window 30 ms, step 10 ms, 40 channels, 125–6 500 Hz, filterbank output shift 7, noise-reduction smoothing (10 bits, 0.025 / 0.06, minimum remaining 0.05), PCAN on (strength 0.95, offset 80, 21 gain bits), log scale shift 6 — because the upstream defaults are different (25 ms window, PCAN off) and relying on them would be a silent mismatch. ps_fe_process() returns at most one frame per call. ps_frontend_diag.cc computes an FNV-1a hash of every table the library builds at init (window, filterbank tables, PCAN lookup, FFT twiddles) and can read or overwrite the two libm-dependent tables — the mechanism behind the M2 parity fix. The int8 conversion used by the model,

static inline int8_t ps_fe_to_int8(uint16_t v) {
    int32_t q = (((int32_t)v * 256) + 333) / 666 - 128;       /* TFLM micro_speech mapping */
    return (int8_t)(q < -128 ? -128 : (q > 127 ? 127 : q));
}

is defined in the header so that the firmware and psfe.py (to_int8) use literally the same arithmetic.

7.3.8 netstream.c — Wi-Fi, TCP and the streamer

  • wifi_init() initialises NVS (erasing it once if the partition holds an incompatible layout — the pre-existing Arduino image left one), creates the default station interface, registers an event handler that reconnects on every disconnect, applies the SSID/password from Kconfig, starts Wi-Fi and disables modem sleep.

  • streamer_task() blocks on a one-slot queue. For each trigger:

7.3.9 serial_link.c and instrument.c — the evidence channel

Frames are A5 5A 26 17 | type | flags | length (u16) | payload | CRC-16/CCITT-FALSE. Types: 1 audio (24-bit samples), 2 text, 3 feature frames, 4 model outputs (capture builds only). The USB write uses a 1 s timeout — a 100 ms timeout once lost a frame when Windows stalled — and a mutex so tasks cannot interleave frames. Text lines are limited to 240 characters (one telemetry line once lost its tail, so @STATS was split into @STATS and @PERF). instrument.c produces @BOOT and @MEM; @MEM reports internal-heap total, free, minimum-ever free and largest block, plus psram=disabled_in_sdkconfig.

7.3.10 cpu_report() — how the CPU figure is produced

Every 10 s the main task calls uxTaskGetSystemState(), which returns each task's accumulated run time. The difference since the last call, divided by the elapsed run-time ticks, gives each task's share; core busy = 100 % − idle task share for IDLE0 and IDLE1. The same line gives the shares of i2s_reader, streamer, netlink, wifi and tcpip_thread. Interrupt time is charged to whichever task was interrupted, so time spent in interrupts during idle is slightly under-counted — a documented limitation (E-43).

7.5 Server code

server/ingest.py (453 lines) is built from five pieces:

  • AsrFrontEnd — conditions audio for the recogniser only: an 8th-order 150 Hz high-pass filter (removes fan rumble) and a fixed gain per stream chosen so that the loudest speech in the pre-roll (the keyword itself, 95th percentile of 50 ms levels in 300–4 000 Hz) sits at −20 dBFS, clamped to 0.5–40×. It holds back the first 571 ms until the gain is known; because the pre-roll arrives as one burst, this adds no measurable delay.
  • VadEndpointer — decides when the command is over: 20 ms frames in 300–4 000 Hz; threshold = keyword level − 20 dB (not below −60 dBFS); speech starts after 3 loud frames; END after 0.7 s of quiet, or after 1.5 s if speech never started.
  • AsrJob — one thread per activation with its own Vosk recogniser, fed through a queue; produces the transcript after the device's END. This is the 29 Sep fix that took ASR off the receive path (E-58).
  • Session — per connection: parses packets, answers PING with PONG using time.perf_counter_ns(), tracks sequence gaps, computes L1_start and L1_audio0 in server time, requests END (only after --min-after-s 0.8 s of post-trigger audio, or at the 6 s cap), and writes files under a lock. Files are named c<HHMMSS>_act<NNNN> with the connection time because the device restarts activation numbers at every reboot and plain names once overwrote ten activations (E-36).
  • serve() / main() — accept loop with a 1 s timeout (so Ctrl+C works on Windows), one thread per connection, model loading, and --print-style compact / --status-file for supervised sessions.

The analysis scripts next to it are the measurement instruments: latency_report.py (L1, lag and L2 per activation — see Chapter 10), stream_integrity.py (finds each server WAV inside the device's own USB recording and checks it sample by sample), replay_session.py (feeds a recorded session through the real Session class to test server changes offline), and asr_level_test.py / make_asr_control.py / asr_offline.py (the controlled ASR experiments of E-45).

8.2 Feature extraction: turning sound into 40 numbers

8.2.1 The idea in simple words

A neural network this small cannot learn from raw samples efficiently. Instead, each 10 ms the device describes the last 30 ms of sound by how much energy there is in each of 40 frequency bands, spaced the way human hearing is (finer at low frequencies, coarser at high ones — the "mel" scale), on a logarithmic scale (so a quiet and a loud "Hey Vayro" look similar). A sequence of these 40-number frames is a picture of the sound — a spectrogram — and the model learns what "Hey Vayro" looks like in that picture.

8.2.2 What the microfrontend does, step by step

  1. Window. Take 480 samples (30 ms) and multiply them by a Hann window, which tapers the ends to zero so the frequency analysis is not polluted by the sharp edges of the cut.
  2. FFT. A 512-point fast Fourier transform (the 480 samples padded to 512) gives the strength of 257 frequencies from 0 to 8 kHz, 31.25 Hz apart. This implementation uses 16-bit fixed-point arithmetic (KissFFT).
  3. Mel filterbank. 40 overlapping triangular filters, from 125 Hz to 6 500 Hz, sum the FFT energies into 40 bands. The 125 Hz floor keeps most of the desk fan's 61–70 Hz rumble out; the 6 500 Hz ceiling stays below the microphone's 6 768 Hz pass-band edge, so training audio and device audio see the same band (the study's option O1).
  4. Noise reduction. For each band, a slowly moving estimate of the background level is subtracted (different smoothing for even and odd bands, keeping at least 5 % of the signal). A steady noise such as a fan fades from the features after a few seconds.
  5. PCAN (per-channel energy normalisation). Each band is divided by a smoothed version of its own recent energy raised to a power (strength 0.95, offset 80). This acts like an automatic gain control per band, so the same word at 50 cm and at 2 m gives similar features.
  6. Log. A fixed-point logarithm compresses the range; the output is 40 unsigned 16-bit numbers. On this device they stayed within 0–631.

The microfrontend is the front end of TensorFlow's own "micro_speech" example, used unmodified. Its noise reduction and PCAN state adapt over time — which is why the training data is built as continuous streams that start 2.5 s before each clip (see Section 8.4.4): the features of a clip depend on the audio before it, exactly as on the always-on device.

Key idea

The design principle is one definition, one implementation, two builds. The same C files produce the device's features (compiled for the Xtensa core) and the training features (compiled for the PC as libpsfe.so), and both read the same frontend.json. The parity test showed the outputs are bit-identical — after one fix (Chapter 11).

8.2.3 From 16-bit features to the model's int8 input

Two mappings happen in sequence. First, ps_fe_to_int8() maps each uint16 feature to int8 with the micro_speech formula (v·256 + 333)/666 − 128 (so the value 666 maps to +128, i.e. ≈ saturation). Second, because the trained model expects its input in its own quantisation (for v2b: scale 0.007567, zero point 4), a 256-entry lookup table, generated from the model's metadata, converts each int8 feature to the model's int8 scale: lut[q] = clip(round(q/128/0.007567 + 4)). Training fed the network q/128 as floats; the lookup table is exactly that scaling, done in integer form. eval_stream.py uses the identical table, so the PC and the device feed the network the same bytes.

8.3 The model: a small depthwise-separable CNN

8.3.1 The concept

A normal convolution layer that turns 24 channels into 32 channels with a 5-tap filter needs 24 × 32 × 5 = 3 840 weights and as many multiply-accumulates per output step. A depthwise-separable convolution splits that into two cheap steps: a depthwise convolution filters each channel on its own (24 × 5 = 120 weights), then a pointwise 1 × 1 convolution mixes channels (24 × 32 = 768 weights) — 888 weights instead of 3 840, with little loss of accuracy. This is the idea behind the DS-CNN keyword-spotting models of Zhang et al. (2017) and many microcontroller models since.

8.3.2 This model

The input is 100 time steps × 1 × 40 values. Although the code uses Keras Conv2D layers, every kernel is k × 1, so the network convolves along time only, and the 40 mel bands act as 40 input channels. In effect it is a 1-D temporal DS-CNN — the "frequency as channels" layout that the study's TC-ResNet discussion describes. VERIFIED build_model()

Points worth understanding:

  • Strides halve time three times (10 → 20 → 40 → 80 ms per step), so the final 6 × 48 feature map summarises the whole 1.02 s window; a single dense layer then produces the score.
  • Two-thirds of the arithmetic is in the first layer (141 120 of 212 584 MACs), because it is the only layer that mixes all 40 input channels at full time resolution.
  • "Valid" (no) padding everywhere. The study's rule X5: a model with padding behaves differently when later converted to a stateful streaming form. By avoiding padding, the same trained weights could later be run incrementally without retraining (DEC-11). VERIFIED code; the conversion itself was not done
  • BatchNorm after every convolution, then ReLU; dropout 0.25 before the output during training only. BatchNorm is folded into the convolutions at export, which is why the int8 file contains only 5 operator types.
  • Training from scratch. No weights are ever loaded; Keras' default initialisers with seed 26172 start every run. This is how the PS's ban on models pre-trained on assistant keywords is met by construction. VERIFIED E-14

8.3.3 Why a windowed model instead of a streaming one

The architecture proposed a stateful streaming model: each new frame updates internal state and the network does only the new work every 30 ms, costing about 1 % of a core. The implementation instead re-runs the network on the whole 100-frame window every 60 ms (DEC-11, approved by the user on 27 Sep). The reason was schedule risk: int8 streaming state in TensorFlow Lite Micro was the least-proven step in the plan and three days remained. The cost is CPU — inference takes about 3.9 % of core 1 instead of ≈ 1 % — which was measured and still fits under 10 %. DOCUMENTED decision; VERIFIED — cost

8.4.4 Mixing and labelling

build_dataset.py never uses a clip on its own. Every example is built as a continuous stream: 2.5 s of a randomly chosen device-recorded bed (scaled by a random −10 to +4 dB, to imitate quieter and louder rooms), then the clip added into the continuing bed at a random signal-to-noise ratio of 0–25 dB (measured in the 125–6 500 Hz band), then 0.4 s more bed. The host twin computes features over the whole stream from a reset state — so, as on the always-on device, noise reduction and PCAN have settled before the clip starts.

Windows of 100 frames are then cut and labelled by the end of the keyword (the study's end-of-keyword labelling, X13):

  • positive: 3 windows whose last frame is 0–160 ms after the keyword's end (chosen at random among the 17 possible);
  • negative "prefix": windows ending 200 ms and 350 ms before the keyword's end — they contain "Hey Vy…" but not the whole word, which teaches the model not to fire early;
  • windows in between are not used (masked);
  • for negative clips: windows ending at, and 80/160 ms after, the clip end, and 200 ms before it;
  • LibriSpeech: a window every 80 ms across the utterance; ambient: every 100 ms across 8 s of bed.

For real recordings (add_real_run) the keywords are located by energy inside each prompt window, positives are taken the same way, and negatives are every 4th window further than 0.3 s before and 1.2 s after any keyword — which includes the spoken confusables and free talk.

Hard-negative mining (v3, v4) is the one step that uses the deployed model's own mistakes: during the overnight and household recordings the device logged its score for every inference; every moment where v2b's live score was ≥ 0.30 in the training hours became a negative example (the window at that frame and ±3 frames, repeated 3 times). These are precisely the sounds the shipped model found keyword-like.

Keyword-then-speech positives (v4 only): half of the synthetic keywords were followed, after a 0–250 ms gap, by 0.5–1.5 s of LibriSpeech speech, because v2b missed keywords that were said straight before a command (single high scores suppressed by the 3-score average, E-51). Labels still mark the keyword's end.

8.7 Runtime inference and decision

The device runs the model every 6 feature frames (60 ms), i.e. 16.7 times per second, on the most recent 100 frames. The output p is averaged over the last three inferences (a 120 ms span), and a trigger fires when the average is at least 0.80 and at least 2 000 ms have passed since the previous trigger. These four numbers (0.80, 3, 6, 2 000) are the "operating point"; they are stored with the model's selection outcome and compiled in through sdkconfig.model.

Why average three scores? A single high score can come from a keyword-like fragment of other speech; a real keyword gives high scores on several consecutive windows as it slides through the 1 s context. Averaging trades a little latency (≈ 60–120 ms) for fewer false triggers — the same trade the study analysed as "smoothing window" (§8.2). It also causes a known failure: a keyword spoken immediately before a command sometimes produced only one high score and was missed (E-51) — the motivation for v4's continuation data.

8.8 Evaluation methodology

8.8.1 Device-identical replay

model/eval/eval_stream.py reproduces the device on the PC: host-twin features over the whole recording from a reset state, the same lookup table, TensorFlow Lite's reference integer kernels (the closest match to the device's integer arithmetic), inference every 6 frames, the same zero-initialised 3-score average, the same threshold and refractory. Because of parity (E-21, E-22), its detections equal the device's live detections — checked explicitly: 27 of 27 trigger times identical on S01 session 2 (E-26), 39 of 39 on the household recording (E-51), and 12 of 12 on the command session (E-57). This is what makes it legitimate to evaluate several models on the same recording, or to replay a recording through a new model. VERIFIED

8.8.2 Scoring keyword sessions

Recording sessions follow a script of prompts (host/scripts/kw_session_v2.txt: 25 "Say: Hey Vayro" prompts at 0.5, 1 and 2 m including fast, slow and soft, then 16 confusable words and 12 s of free talk). Two scorers exist:

  • Prompt score (primary): a keyword prompt is a hit if any detection falls between 0.3 s after the prompt starts and 1.5 s after it ends; a detection inside a confusable, talk or silence prompt (and not in that 1.5 s spill-over) is a false activation. It needs no labelling and tolerates speakers who start late. It was introduced after session 2 showed speakers drifting into the next prompt (DEC-20).
  • Labeller score (secondary): locates each spoken keyword by energy in 300–4 000 Hz (20 ms frames, threshold = the prompt window's 20th percentile + 9 dB, gaps up to 0.3 s bridged, at most 2 s long) and measures the detection lag after the keyword's acoustic end.

8.8.3 False activations per hour

Continuous recordings in which nobody said the keyword are replayed and every detection is a false activation. The result is always reported with its exposure (hours) and, where the count is small, an upper bound (for zero events in T hours, the "rule of three": fewer than 3/T per hour at 95 % confidence). Household recordings that include a test where the keyword was said have those minutes excluded.

8.8.4 Declared splits and selection rules

Before each new model, the data split and the rule for choosing it were written to a file and committed before the results existed: sources.json (the night split declared before the night was recorded; the household split declared before v4 was built) and model/artifacts/selection_rule_v2/v3/v4.json. The outcomes are recorded next to them. Chapter 10 tells the history, including the two occasions when a rule was not followed and why.

8.10 The recogniser side

The server does three things to the audio before and around recognition:

  1. Conditioning (AsrFrontEnd): device speech arrives quiet (around −40 dBFS at 30–50 cm) on top of fan rumble at about −30 dBFS. A controlled test showed the recogniser turned a clean LibriSpeech sentence at that level with the room's background into the single word "that"; after an 8th-order 150 Hz high-pass filter and gain it decoded almost perfectly (E-45). The gain is set once per stream from the keyword's level in the pre-roll (speech → −20 dBFS), because a moving gain changed transcripts between near-identical takes (E-48).
  2. Endpointing (VadEndpointer): an energy detector in the speech band ends the stream after 0.7 s of quiet following speech, or 1.5 s if no speech follows the keyword; never before 0.8 s; at most 6 s. It replaced Vosk's own end-of-utterance rule, which on this audio waited for its 5 s silence timeout and kept streams open so long that the next keywords could not be streamed (E-48).
  3. Recognition: Vosk with the en-in-0.5 Indian-English model (Kaldi-based, not a transformer), open vocabulary, word timestamps. The words that start before the end of the pre-roll are the keyword; the rest is reported as "after trigger" — the command. A 12-phrase grammar mode exists (--grammar server/commands.json) but was a development experiment and is not the validated configuration.

9.9 Deployment restrictions (as built)

  • The ASR server must run on a PC on the same LAN, at the compiled-in address, awake (a sleeping PC killed the server once, E-58).
  • Without a PC reading the USB port, boot waits 30 s before listening.
  • No over-the-air update, no provisioning interface, no persistent configuration other than the compiled-in values.
  • One stream at a time per device; the server accepts several connections (one thread each) but was only ever tested with one device.
  • Plain TCP without authentication: suitable for a trusted LAN only.

10.4 Phase 1 — Physical audio (26–27 Sep)

10.4.1 I2S alignment sweep (E-18)

  • Objective: confirm that the samples arriving at the chip are the microphone's samples, bit for bit.
  • Hypothesis: Philips framing, as the study's compatibility table specified (P-02).
  • Method: at every boot the firmware captures 0.3 s with each of four configurations (Philips / MSB-justified × normal / inverted bit clock) and reports, per slot, the fraction of samples whose 24-bit least significant bit is 1 (≈ 50 % for real audio; 0 % if the data arrives one bit late) and whether the empty slot stays empty.
  • Result: Philips: LSB = 1 in 0.00 % of samples, and 3.3 % of the empty slot's words non-zero (sign bits leaking in). MSB-justified: LSB = 1 in 50.85 %, empty slot 100 % zero. Inverted clocks gave the same verdicts. Repeated on two boots. VERIFIED runs/20260926_214456_m1_align_sweep_warm/device_log.txt
  • Status: hypothesis rejected; design changed to MSB-justified framing (DEC-06). Root cause (sampling edge or WS timing) not isolated.
  • Why it matters: with the wrong framing every sample is halved and loses its lowest bit — the audio still sounds right, so the error would have survived into training and deployment unnoticed.

10.4.2 EXP-10: does mono mode halve DMA traffic? (E-05)

  • Method: measure bytes per second delivered by DMA in stereo and in mono mode.
  • Result: stereo 127 963–127 965 B/s; mono 63 976 B/s (4 boots, identical). VERIFIED
  • Status: pass — mono is used, which also removes the empty slot from the capture path.

10.4.3 Start-up blanking and settling (E-04, E-19)

  • Hypothesis: the datasheet's 2^18-clock zero output after power-up (≈ 256 ms).
  • Result: no zeros — the first non-zero word came 0.56 ms after the clock started, on 3 warm boots and 1 cold power-up. Instead, a DC offset of up to 26 % of full scale decays over ≈ 1.5 s; in the 125–6 500 Hz feature band the level is at the room floor after 100 ms. DOCUMENTED
  • Status: the study's 300 ms settle time is enough for the features; a proposed 2–3 s settle (C-11) was withdrawn (DEC-10). The sample (one cold boot) is small.

10.4.4 Levels and pass band (E-02, E-03, E-06)

  • Method: scripted cold-boot session A: silence, speech at 0.5 m, a steady "shhh", speech at 1 m; spectra and 100 ms levels.
  • Result: feature-band room floor −52.9 dBFS (fan on); words at −38…−42 dBFS peaks; the 6 768–8 000 Hz band 15.8 dB below 4 000–6 768 Hz. DOCUMENTED spectrum re-plotted in Figure 4.1
  • Interpretation: the level is low but usable; keeping the top 16 bits (>> 16) is fine (DEC-09); the mel ceiling at 6 500 Hz is justified by the measured roll-off.

10.4.5 Capture-link integrity (E-07, E-20, E-40)

  • Objective: make sure recordings used for training and testing have no hidden gaps.
  • Findings and fixes: (1) a 100 ms USB write timeout lost frames → 1 s; (2) the Windows USB serial driver ignored a bare RTS pulse, so the board was not actually reset and a stalled link persisted → esptool's DTR/RTS sequence and a check that @BOOT arrives before audio is accepted; (3) a "USB connected?" check in the firmware briefly read false while the host was reading and dropped frames → removed; (4) a host-side stall watchdog that auto-resets into a new session. DOCUMENTED
  • Result after fixes: 60 s clean, then the overnight run: 36 000 s, 0 CRC errors, 0 I2S overflows, 1 gap of 20 ms (320 samples), 0 auto-resets. VERIFIED ambient_night_20260927/capture_summary.json

10.5 Phase 2 — Front-end parity (27 Sep)

10.5.1 M2 golden-vector parity: first attempt fails (E-21)

  • Objective: prove the device computes exactly the features the model will be trained on (study §7.3; failure modes X3, X4).
  • Method: the device streams its int16 audio and its 40-value feature frames over USB; the PC recomputes the features from the same audio with the host twin (same C sources); frames are compared value by value, and the front-end SHA-256 must match.
  • Result (60 s): 5 995 of 5 998 frames identical; 3 values in 3 frames differed (bands 34–35, max difference 26). FAIL. VERIFIED runs/20260927_134200_m2_parity_60s/parity_report.json
  • Investigation: a per-table hash diagnostic (@FETAB) showed the filterbank weights differ: they are computed at start-up with log1pf(), and the chip's C library (newlib) rounds differently from the PC's (glibc). The PCAN lookup table's first comparison was inconclusive because the hash included never-initialised slots — the hash was fixed to cover only the entries the code reads.
  • Change: the host twin's tables are exported to ps_frontend_tables.c and copied into the device's state after initialisation, if their recorded JSON hash matches (DEC-16).
  • Result after the fix: 12 000/12 000 frames bit-exact over 120 s and 3 001/3 001 in a second run; 8/8 table hashes equal. PASS. VERIFIED

Chapter 11 Debugging, Failures and Engineering Iterations

Chapter 10 told the story in time order. This chapter takes the most important technical problems one at a time and answers the same eight questions for each: what was observed, under what conditions, the suspected cause, how it was investigated, what evidence confirmed or rejected the cause, what was changed, what happened after, and whether it is resolved. Where the root cause was never proven, it says so.

11.1 Problem 1 — Audio captured one bit late

  1. Observed: with the planned Philips I2S framing, the lowest bit of every 24-bit sample was always 0, and the supposedly empty right slot contained small non-zero values.
  2. Conditions: every boot, warm or cold; both bit-clock polarities.
  3. Suspected cause: the receiver samples the data one bit-clock later than the microphone presents it — a framing mismatch between the INMP441 and the S3's Philips mode on this board.
  4. Investigation: a built-in sweep over four framings, measuring two signatures that do not depend on what sound is present: the LSB-is-1 fraction (≈ 50 % for real audio) and the empty-slot contents (must be all zero).
  5. Evidence: Philips 0.00 % LSB, 3.3 % non-zero words in the empty slot; MSB-justified 50.85 %, 0 %. Repeated on two boots. VERIFIED
  6. Change: MSB-justified framing, locked in sdkconfig.board (DEC-06); runs recorded with Philips framing are excluded from training (sources.json).
  7. After: every later recording shows correct statistics.
  8. Status: resolved in behaviour; root cause unconfirmed — whether it is the S3's sampling edge, the WS timing or the breakout was not isolated (no logic analyser).

11.2 Problem 2 — The evidence link lost data

  1. Observed: single audio frames missing; once the board was never reset and a stalled link persisted; later, bursts of lost audio during long captures.
  2. Conditions: Windows host; native USB; heavy PC load.
  3. Suspected causes: timeouts too short for Windows USB scheduling; the Windows USB serial driver not forwarding a lone RTS change; a firmware "is USB connected?" check reading false while the host was actually reading; host CPU starvation.
  4. Investigation: sample-index gap detection in capture.py (every audio frame carries its first sample index), CRC checks, device-side drop counters (pool_drops, link_drops), and correlating stalls with host activity.
  5. Evidence: each fix removed its symptom; the overnight run then had 0 CRC errors and a single 20 ms gap in 10 h; later stalls coincided exactly with unzipping and loading the 1 GB ASR model (E-50). VERIFIED / DOCUMENTED
  6. Changes: 1 s write timeout; esptool's DTR/RTS reset sequence plus waiting for @BOOT; removed the connection gate; 1 MB host receive buffer and high process priority; a stall watchdog that resets and starts a new, separately reported session; missing samples written as zeros and reported, never hidden.
  7. After: clean multi-hour recordings.
  8. Status: resolved, with an operating rule: no heavy PC work during long captures.

11.3 Problem 3 — Device features did not exactly match training features

  1. Observed: 3 values in 3 of 5 998 frames differed between the device and the host twin (bands 34–35, up to 26 units).
  2. Conditions: same audio, same C sources, same frontend.json.
  3. Suspected cause: the only thing not shared is the platform's maths library: tables computed at start-up with floating-point functions (log1pf for the filterbank, powf for PCAN, cos/sin for the window and FFT) might round differently on the chip (newlib) and the PC (glibc).
  4. Investigation: hash every start-up table on both sides and compare (@FETAB, compare_tables.py); fix a hash that included uninitialised lookup-table slots (it had made the PCAN comparison meaningless).
  5. Evidence: the filterbank weights differed; the other tables matched once hashed correctly. DOCUMENTED DEC-16; final 8/8 equality VERIFIED
  6. Change: export the host's tables (export_tables.py → ps_frontend_tables.c) and overwrite the device's copies after initialisation, guarded by the front-end JSON hash (DEC-16). The host's tables are the "canonical" ones because the training features were computed with them.
  7. After: 12 000/12 000 and 3 001/3 001 frames bit-exact; model outputs 1 984/1 984 bit-exact.
  8. Status: resolved. A three-in-six-thousand difference sounds harmless, but it would have made every later "device equals replay" comparison approximate instead of exact.

11.4 Problem 4 — The model's first int8 export contained unsupported-looking operators

  1. Observed: the converted model contained SHAPE, STRIDED_SLICE and PACK operators besides the expected ones.
  2. Cause (confirmed by the fix): Keras 3 keeps a dynamic batch dimension through Reshape/Flatten, and the converter lowers that to shape arithmetic at run time.
  3. Change: export.py rebuilds the network with batch_size=1, copies the trained weights and converts that static graph (DEC-17); the Keras Flatten was replaced by a Reshape with a static size.
  4. After: only CONV_2D, DEPTHWISE_CONV_2D, RESHAPE, FULLY_CONNECTED, LOGISTIC; identical outputs on S01; the firmware registers exactly these five kernels. VERIFIED
  5. Status: resolved. A related detail: TensorFlow's CPU depthwise kernel requires equal strides, so the code writes strides (s, s); with a kernel width of 1 and input width 1 this is identical to (s, 1) (comment in train.py).

11.5 Problem 5 — The synthetic voices said the wrong word

  1. Observed: Piper's English voices pronounced "Hey Vayro" as "Hey VAY-ro" instead of "Hey VAI-ro".
  2. Investigation: print each voice's phonemes for candidate spellings (tts_probe.py) and write sample WAVs for listening.
  3. Evidence: "Hey Vayro" → /veɪɹoʊ/ in all three English voices; "Hey Vyro" → /vaɪɹoʊ/; Hindi "हे वाइरो" → /ʋaːɪɾoː/. DOCUMENTED DEC-14
  4. Change: the synthesiser is given "Hey Vyro" / "हे वाइरो"; the keyword itself is unchanged.
  5. Status: resolved by phonemes; the planned human listening check was not recorded as done. UNVERIFIED

11.6 Problem 6 — Validation on synthetic data picked the worse model

  1. Observed: v1 beat v0 on the synthetic validation set (PR-AUC 0.949 vs 0.929) but detected fewer real keywords (18/23 vs 22/23).
  2. Cause: synthetic speech is a different distribution from a real person through this microphone in this room (failure mode X16 in the study).
  3. Change: a separate real validation session (S01 session 2) for all selection, a different real session for testing, and selection rules declared in files before results exist (DEC-18, DEC-19).
  4. Status: resolved as a method. It is also the root of the v2 rule amendment and the DEC-26 override (Problem 11): with very little real data, every selection decision is fragile.

11.7 Problem 7 — Labelling real keywords automatically was unreliable

  1. Observed: the first energy-based labeller produced impossible keyword spans (0.02 s, 2.7 s) under fan noise; in session 2, speakers drifted to the end of their 2.5 s prompt windows and into the next prompt, so detections were credited to the wrong prompt (labeller recall 15/25 vs prompt recall 20/25).
  2. Changes: labeller v2 (speech band 300–4 000 Hz, a threshold relative to each window's own floor, gap bridging, a 2 s cap); recording protocol v2 with 4 s windows and a "next:" preview (DEC-20); a label-free prompt score as the primary metric.
  3. Status: mitigated. Keyword ends located by energy still have 20 ms resolution and can latch onto a command spoken with no pause (which made one latency session unusable, E-62).

11.8 Problem 8 — Results misread before checking what was said

Two separate times, a result was first read as a failure and later corrected once the user confirmed what had actually been spoken: the 27 Sep "no detection in the evening" session (no keyword had been said, E-33), and the 28 Sep "ASR returns nothing" session (no command had been said; one stream held singing from another room, E-45, E-47). In between, a Wi-Fi-interference hypothesis for the evening noise was tested with a controlled on/off A/B and rejected (E-34). Change: ground truth is confirmed with the person before a result is interpreted, and both ledger entries were rewritten as explicit corrections that state the original, wrong interpretation. Status: resolved as practice. DOCUMENTED

11.9 Problem 9 — The first product build did not fit in 256 KB

  1. Observed: S4 = 266 483 B (260.2 KiB) — over both readings of the limit.
  2. Conditions: the first "product" configuration with Wi-Fi, TCP, the model and the ring.
  3. Suspected cause (identified two days earlier as C-10): the architecture's budget omitted code placed in internal RAM — ESP-IDF puts interrupt handlers, parts of FreeRTOS, the heap allocator and, by default, performance-critical Wi-Fi paths in IRAM, and under the strict reading that RAM counts. The capture-mode extras (a 64-slot USB transmit pool, a 40 KiB arena, a 1.0 s ring) added more.
  4. Investigation: the linker's section sizes (idf.py size), per-step heap reports (@MEM after each initialisation step), and the stack high-water marks.
  5. Evidence: IRAM .text was 60 015 B in the M1 build and 92 575 B in the first network build; the heap steps attributed 12 040 B to the front end and 53 060 B to Wi-Fi/lwIP/network tasks (E-17, E-29, E-42). DOCUMENTED
  6. Changes (DEC-22): move Wi-Fi, FreeRTOS, ring-buffer and heap functions from IRAM to flash; capture streams off; arena sized to need (16 KiB); ring and pre-roll shortened using the study's own table (justified by the measured 144 ms worst-case lag); Wi-Fi/lwIP buffer trims; model input written in place; reader stack trimmed after measuring its high-water mark.
  7. After: 243 767 B not streaming (E-42), then 259 255 B while streaming (E-52) — still 3 255 B over 256 000 — then DEC-24 (heap poisoning off, no A-MPDU, 8 TX buffers, no IPv6) → 249 615 B (E-53), final 250 899 B (E-60). VERIFIED
  8. Status: resolved with ≈ 5 kB of margin on the strict reading. Trade-off: code executed from flash competes with model weights for the cache, which is the most likely reason inference gets slower when Wi-Fi is busy INFERRED (Chapter 12).

11.10 Problem 10 — The speech recogniser heard nothing useful

  1. Observed: empty or nonsense transcripts; Vosk ended utterances only at its 5 s silence limit; long streams hid the next keyword; transcripts flipped between near-identical takes; the first stream after start-up had an extra ≈ 25–40 ms delay.
  2. Conditions: device speech around −40 dBFS at 30–50 cm with desk-fan rumble around −30 dBFS; the small en-in-0.4 model; open vocabulary.
  3. Suspected causes: level and low-frequency noise far from what the recogniser was trained on; a gain control that moved inside words; a weak model; the recogniser's own endpointing unsuitable; lazy library imports.
  4. Investigation: a controlled experiment (make_asr_control.py, asr_level_test.py): one LibriSpeech sentence clean, then at the device's level with this room's recorded background, then with high-pass filtering and gain; and replay_session.py, which feeds recorded sessions through the real server code so variants can be compared on identical audio.
  5. Evidence: clean → correct; device level + background → "that"; + 150 Hz 8th-order high-pass + gain → correct (E-45). Replay reproduced the live transcripts exactly and showed that gain rule, ASR start offset and recogniser persistence each flipped individual results — the small model was fragile (E-48). DOCUMENTED
  6. Changes: AsrFrontEnd (high-pass + one fixed gain per stream, referenced to the keyword in the pre-roll); the larger en-in-0.5 model; VadEndpointer with its rule fixed before evaluation; the recogniser pre-created and numpy/scipy imported at model load.
  7. After: end to end 2/10 in a noisy live test with the TV on (E-49), 3/4 exact in the demonstration (E-61). The user's listening check showed the delivered audio was intelligible, so the remaining errors are the recogniser's.
  8. Status: partially resolved. Delivery of audio is solid; recognition accuracy is limited by an off-the-shelf model (publisher's WER 36 %).

11.11 Problem 11 — The ASR server caused stream overruns

  1. Observed: 3 of 31 streams overran and 1 timed out with the ASR server; 0 of 31 with the plain server on the same firmware.
  2. Suspected cause: the recogniser ran inside the server's receive loop; while it decoded, the server stopped reading the socket; TCP flow control pushed back to the device; the streamer could not send; the ring filled.
  3. Investigation: replay timing of Vosk calls (stalls up to ≈ 0.6 s), and the device's own minimum-slack figure per stream (the 0.75 s ring leaves ≈ 180 ms beyond the 571 ms pre-roll).
  4. Evidence: the arithmetic fits (a 0.6 s stall exceeds 180 ms of slack); the fix removed the symptom. DOCUMENTED cause; fix VERIFIED
  5. Change: AsrJob — one recognition thread per stream fed by a queue; the receive thread only timestamps, runs the VAD and queues.
  6. After: 30/30 streams clean with the ASR server (E-60); 12/12 in T1. VERIFIED
  7. Status: resolved. The episode showed that the device's OVERRUN design works: the damaged streams were flagged and ended short, never delivered as corrupted audio.

11.12 Problem 12 — False triggers in an active household

  7. Status: open — the main accuracy limitation of the system.

13.2 Level A — Conceptual contributions

13.2.1 A1. Making every requirement falsifiable before building

The challenge. The PS's limits ("< 256 KB RAM", "< 10 % CPU", "near-zero false activations", "latency from keyword end") are ambiguous enough that almost any system could be described as compliant.

Why the ordinary approach falls short. The usual practice is to report the most favourable number — model size instead of total RAM, one core's inference time instead of system CPU, detection-to-send time instead of keyword end to server, accuracy on a clean test set instead of false triggers per hour.

The choice made. Adopt the strictest defensible reading of each requirement before measuring, and measure it with instruments the reader can re-run: S4 includes IRAM code, every stack and the Wi-Fi buffers with PSRAM disabled, and is checked against both kB and KiB; CPU is reported per core and averaged; latency starts at the keyword's acoustic end; false triggers are reported per condition with exposure hours; selection rules and data splits are committed before the data they judge exists.

Evidence of usefulness. It changed outcomes: the strict memory reading exposed the IRAM omission and forced real engineering (260 → 245 KiB); the strict latency reading replaced a flattering 95 ms with the measured 138 ms; the declared rules made two departures from them visible and documented instead of silent (the v2 amendment and DEC-26). What remains unproven: that evaluators read the requirements the same way. Credit: the framing (nested scopes, three latency definitions, kill conditions) is the architecture study's; the execution and the evidence are this project's.

13.2.2 A2. Privacy as a property of the data flow

The device transmits nothing but clock-sync packets until a detection, and the stream is bounded (571 ms before, the command, a hard 10 s cap). This is a design property that can be checked in code (ns_trigger is the only path that starts a stream). The project also states the honest corollary: a false trigger sends 2–6 s of room audio, so privacy is exactly as good as the false-trigger rate. VERIFIED

13.3 Level B — Architectural contributions

13.3.1 B1. A front end that is bit-identical on the chip and in training

The challenge. A keyword model learns the exact statistics of its input features. If the device computes them even slightly differently from the training pipeline, accuracy degrades silently — the study lists this as failure modes X3 and X4.

Why the ordinary approach falls short. Typical pipelines compute training features with a Python library (librosa, TensorFlow ops) and device features with C code, and check them only by eye or with a tolerance. Even a shared library can differ across platforms, as this project found.

The choice made and how it works. (1) One JSON file defines every feature parameter; the firmware compiles it into a header, the training code reads it at run time, and both carry its SHA-256. (2) The same C source files — the vendored microfrontend and KissFFT — are compiled for the Xtensa core and for the PC (libpsfe.so, called from Python through ctypes). (3) Because the chip's maths library computes two start-up tables differently from the PC's, the PC's tables are exported and copied into the device at boot, again guarded by the JSON hash. (4) The model records the front-end hash its training data used, and the firmware refuses to run a model built for a different front end.

Components. frontend.json, gen_frontend_config.py, psfe.py, build_twin.sh, export_tables.py, ps_frontend_diag.cc, parity_test.py, kws_init().

Trade-offs. It rules out the faster float esp-dsp FFT the study wanted (the FFT must be the same code on both sides), costing CPU; the canonical tables add 1 402 bytes of flash (288 + 288 + 125 16-bit values).

Evidence. 12 000/12 000 feature frames and 1 984/1 984 model outputs bit-exact; live device triggers identical to offline replay on three separate recordings (27/27, 39/39, 12/12). VERIFIED Unproven: parity was tested on room audio and speech at normal levels; extreme levels (clipping) were not specifically exercised.

Why it matters. It converts the device into something that can be simulated exactly. Every accuracy number in this report — including comparisons of models that were never flashed — rests on this.

13.3.2 B2. One ring buffer, sized from measurement, that fails safe

The challenge. The device knows the keyword was said only after it ends, so it must keep recent audio; memory is scarce; the stream must never deliver corrupted audio if the network stalls.

The choice made. A single int16 ring written once per hop on core 1 and read by the streamer on core 0; positions are sample counts since boot, so the pre-roll start is w − 9 136 and "was I overtaken?" is one subtraction; release/acquire atomics instead of a lock; an explicit OVERRUN message and a short stream when the reader is lapped. The ring was then shrunk from 1.0 s to 0.75 s using the study's own table, once the measured worst-case detection lag (144 ms) justified it.

Trade-offs. Only 179 ms of slack beyond the pre-roll — which is exactly why the server's blocking recogniser caused overruns (Problem 11).

Evidence. 0 overruns in 30/30 and 12/12 final streams; the 3 overruns during the server fault were all flagged, none delivered as corrupted audio; 23/23 streams bit-exact against the device's own audio. VERIFIED Credit: the unified-ring idea and the pre-roll derivation are the study's (§8.3–§8.5); the measured sizing, index arithmetic and the evidence are this project's.

13.3.3 B3. Measuring "keyword end → server" without laboratory equipment

The challenge. The PS's latency starts at the moment the keyword acoustically ends — an event neither computer timestamps. The study's answer was a loudspeaker rig that raises a GPIO at a known sample, plus a logic analyser; neither was available.

The choice made. Split the path into three pieces and measure each where it is observable: (1) the device's detection time converted to server time by an NTP-style offset carried over the same warm TCP connection (±1.2–1.8 ms); (2) the server's receive time of the first audio packet; (3) the keyword's end located inside the streamed audio itself — possible precisely because the stream starts 571 ms before the detection — and measured back from the detection point. The one unobservable piece (last captured sample → decision, ≈ 10 ms) is stated as an estimate with an error bar rather than ignored.

Evidence. The method reproduced itself exactly when re-run (E-37 recomputed identically on 30 Sep) and gives the full per-activation breakdown in Figure 10.5. Unproven: the 10 ± 7 ms term; energy-based keyword-end location fails when a command follows with no pause (one session was unusable, E-62). Why it matters: it produces an L2 figure whose every component is either measured or explicitly estimated.

13.3.4 B4. Core partitioning that makes the CPU claim clean

All work that happens every 10 ms — capture, conversion, ring, features, inference, decision — runs in one task pinned to core 1 at priority 20; Wi-Fi, TCP, streaming and telemetry run on core 0. Besides protecting the audio path from Wi-Fi interrupt storms, this makes the "idle listening" CPU of the pipeline directly visible as core 1's busy time, and per-stage cycle counters inside that task attribute it. The study proposed this split (§4.8); the implementation simplified it from three tasks to one. VERIFIED Trade-off: core 1's margin (≈ 0.5 point) is thin because everything is concentrated there; unproven: whether moving the front end to core 0 would help overall.

13.4 Level C — Implementation contributions

13.4.1 C1. A closed-loop data engine built on the device's own mistakes

The challenge. A new keyword has no public dataset, the team had one speaker at first, and household sound caused false triggers that no synthetic data represented.

Why the ordinary approach falls short. Typical custom-wake-word pipelines mix clean clips into generic noise datasets; the model never sees this microphone, this room, or the specific sounds that fool it.

The choice made and how it works.

  • Everything is mixed into audio recorded through the product's own microphone in the target room (beds), at randomised SNR and room gain.
  • Every example is a continuous stream with a 2.5 s lead-in, so the front end's adaptive noise reduction and gain control are in the same state as on the always-on device when the keyword arrives.
  • Labels are placed by the keyword's end, with "prefix" negatives that teach the model not to fire on "Hey Vy…".
  • The deployed model's own live scores — logged for every inference during hours of real household audio — are used to find the moments it found keyword-like (score ≥ 0.30), and those moments become weighted negatives for the next model. Only declared training hours are mined; held-out hours stay untouched.

Evidence — including where it did not work. The live logs made v3 and v4 possible within hours. Mining quiet-night mistakes (v3) made household false triggers worse (19.9 vs 15.8 /h); mining household TV/talk mistakes (v4) made them better (9 vs 13 in the held-out hour) but hurt a new speaker's recall (14/25 vs 20/25). VERIFIED Interpretation: the loop works as a mechanism; its data must contain the right kind of negatives and more than one speaker's positives. Unproven: that the loop converges with more speakers — the obvious next experiment.

13.4.2 C2. Fitting the strictest RAM reading through configuration engineering

The challenge. The strict S4 reading counts IRAM code and all Wi-Fi/TCP/IP buffers, which the architecture's budget had missed; the first product build was 4.3 KB over 262 144 B and 10.5 KB over 256 000 B.

The choice made. Attribute every byte (linker sections + heap reports after each initialisation step), then remove what the product does not need rather than shrinking the model: move Wi-Fi, FreeRTOS, ring-buffer and heap code from IRAM to flash; size the arena from arena_used_bytes(); size the ring from the measured lag; write the model input in place; trim Wi-Fi/lwIP buffers and disable features a 32 kB/s uplink does not use (A-MPDU, IPv6, heap poisoning).

Evidence. 266 483 → 243 767 (not streaming) → 259 255 (streaming, over) → 249 615 → final 250 899 B; every step measured and recomputed. VERIFIED Trade-offs: code from flash competes with model weights for cache, making inference time vary with Wi-Fi traffic INFERRED; ≈ 5 kB of margin remains. Credit: the "network stack and RTOS, not the model, dominate" insight is the study's; the IRAM finding (C-10) and the trims are this project's.

13.4.3 C3. ASR conditioning referenced to the keyword

The challenge. Device speech arrives quiet and on top of fan rumble; a moving gain changed transcripts between near-identical takes; the recogniser's own end-of-utterance rule did not work on this audio.

The choice made. The pre-roll always contains the keyword, spoken by the same person at the same distance just before the command. The server uses it twice: to set one fixed gain for the whole stream (keyword speech → −20 dBFS, after a 150 Hz high-pass), and to set the VAD threshold relative to the keyword's level (keyword − 20 dB). Recognition runs in its own thread so it can never block reception.

Evidence. The controlled experiment (a LibriSpeech sentence: correct → "that" at device level → correct after conditioning, E-45); 30/30 clean streams after the thread change; demonstration 3/4 commands exact. Unproven: a systematic word-error-rate comparison with and without conditioning on real commands (only small sessions exist).

13.4.4 C4. Compliance as code

A build cannot complete if any vendor wake-word SDK or known pre-trained wake-word model is referenced anywhere in the repository (forbidden_deps.py, run by the build wrapper); the closed Wi-Fi/PHY libraries were listed from the linker map, not from memory; "trained from scratch" is checkable in train.py; two non-commercial training voices are disclosed with the retraining path. The study recommended the gate (X25); the project automated it. VERIFIED

14.4 Physically tested versus tested in software only

Physically tested on the device: microphone path and framing; feature parity (device features compared with the PC); model output parity; live detection on scripted sessions (S01 sessions 2 and 3, S02, command sessions, T1, demonstration); 10 h and 2.5 h live listening; memory and CPU on the product build; streaming, overruns, latency; network outage behaviour.

Tested in software only (offline replay): every comparison between models (v0–v4) other than the one running live at the time; false-activation rates of models that were not on the device during the recording; threshold sweeps. These are legitimate because live-equals-replay was verified on three recordings — but they are still simulations of the device, not the device.

14.5 What has not been verified

  • The capture-term portion of latency (≈ 10 ms, estimated).
  • Behaviour on a congested or different network; L3 (catch-up) latency.
  • Any speaker other than S01 and S02; any room other than one home; children's voices; accents beyond those represented by the TTS voices.
  • Power consumption.
  • Long-duration stability of the product build (the longest product run is 12 minutes; the 10 h run used the capture build).
  • The physical board details (maker, breadboard, pull-down resistor).

14.6 Known limitations

  1. False triggers with television or conversation (15.8 /h) — roughly one every four minutes in a lively household; each sends a few seconds of room audio to the server.
  2. Speaker coverage — one speaker in training; one additional speaker tested.
  3. Thin CPU margin on core 1 (≈ 0.5 point idle) and > 10 % while streaming; inference time varies with Wi-Fi activity.
  4. Thin RAM margin (≈ 5 kB on the 256 000 B reading).
  5. Fixed network configuration — server address and Wi-Fi credentials are compiled in; no encryption or authentication.
  6. ASR quality — the recogniser is an off-the-shelf open model; command recognition is not reliable enough for real control.
  7. Latency evidence is modest — 12 activations, one speaker, one session.
  8. Boot behaviour — 30 s wait without a USB host; ≈ 3 s of microphone diagnostics on every boot.

14.7 Dependencies required to run the system

Hardware: the ESP32-S3 board with the INMP441 wired as in Table 4.5; a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi network; a PC on the same LAN. Software: ESP-IDF 5.5.5 and the pinned components to rebuild; Python 3.12 with numpy, scipy and vosk 0.3.45, and the 1 GB Vosk en-in-0.5 model, for the server; the WSL training environment only to retrain.

14.8 What would have to change for larger-scale deployment

  • Robustness of detection across many speakers, rooms and televisions — a much larger real dataset and a held-out test set of many speakers; possibly a second-stage verifier on the server that can reject false triggers (the audio is already there).
  • Configuration and provisioning — Wi-Fi and server settings stored in NVS and set without rebuilding; server discovery; no dependence on a USB host.
  • Security — authenticated, encrypted transport (TLS established at boot, as the study advised, with the RAM budget re-checked); no credentials in the firmware image.
  • Fleet operations — over-the-air updates, device identity, remote logging, crash reporting.
  • Server — a scalable ASR service (many concurrent streams), monitoring, and a proper command-understanding layer.
  • Hardware — a production PCB with the microphone's recommended pull-down and decoupling, and measured power in the intended power mode.
Caution

Prototype, not product. Everything in this report shows that the architecture works on the chosen hardware within the PS's limits, with honest measurements. It does not show that the device is ready for real homes: the false-trigger rate in normal household conditions, the two-speaker evidence, the fixed network configuration and the absence of security are each individually disqualifying for a product.

Chapter 15 Future Technical Development

Every item in this chapter is FUTURE: not implemented, not tested. Each follows from a limitation measured in Chapters 12 and 14, and is written so that it can be picked up as a work package: the limitation it addresses, the change, the subsystem, the resources, how to validate it, and what it could break.

15.1 Reduce household false triggers without losing new speakers

  • Addresses: 15.8 false triggers per hour with TV and conversation; v4's loss of recall on a new speaker.
  • Change: record keyword sessions from 5–10 more speakers (different ages, accents, distances) and several hours of household audio from more than one home; retrain the same architecture with speaker-disjoint splits; mine hard negatives from household hours only; keep positives balanced across speakers.
  • Subsystem: data and training (model/data, model/train); no firmware change unless the operating point moves.
  • Resources: volunteers, the existing capture tool and scripts, ≈ 1 h of training per model on the current PC.
  • Validation: declare the rule first (as with v3/v4) and include a new-speaker criterion; report recall per speaker and false triggers per condition with exposure; confirm live-equals-replay on at least one new recording.
  • Risks: more negatives can narrow the keyword (the v4 effect); new speakers must never leak into the test set.

15.2 Use the operating curve that already exists in the recordings

  • Addresses: the choice of N = 3 and threshold 0.80 was made on one speaker's validation session.
  • Change: because replay is exact, sweep the averaging length (3–10 scores), the threshold, and a two-threshold rule on the existing ≈ 13 h of recordings, and plot false triggers per hour against recall and against latency — the study's EXP-11 "latency-vs-FA/h operating curve".
  • Subsystem: evaluation only (eval_stream.py already supports threshold and N sweeps).
  • Validation: choose the point on validation data only; confirm on fresh recordings.
  • Risks: longer averaging adds latency (≈ 60 ms per extra score) and more pre-roll memory.

15.3 Convert the model to stateful streaming

  • Addresses: inference costs ≈ 3.9 % of core 1 because the whole 1 s window is recomputed every 60 ms (DEC-11); the busiest-core margin is ≈ 0.5 point.
  • Change: convert the trained "valid-padding" network to a streaming form that keeps each layer's recent outputs and computes only the new time steps (the reason X5 was followed), then run it every 30–60 ms.
  • Subsystem: model/train (conversion), kws_engine (state buffers), app_main.c (scheduling).
  • Validation: streaming outputs must equal windowed outputs on recorded audio (a new parity test), then EXP-2 again.
  • Risks: extra RAM for layer state (must be re-measured against the 5 kB margin); TFLM support for the required state operators.

15.4 Measure what is still estimated

  • Addresses: the 10 ± 7 ms capture term; the unused GPIO instruments; n = 12 latency sample.
  • Change: a logic analyser on GPIO15/GPIO16 and a loudspeaker playback rig that marks the keyword's end on a third channel (the study's IN-1/IN-2); ≥ 100 activations at several distances and volumes.
  • Subsystem: test harness only.
  • Validation: the hardware path and the software path must agree within their stated uncertainty.
  • Risks: none to the product; loudspeaker playback is a different acoustic source from a person.

15.5 Measure and reduce power

  • Addresses: power has never been measured; Wi-Fi modem sleep is disabled.
  • Change: measure current in idle listening and while streaming (USB power meter or shunt); then test modem sleep and a lower CPU clock, re-measuring latency and CPU.
  • Validation: current in mA with conditions; the latency and CPU tests repeated.
  • Risks: modem sleep delays server messages (the reason it was disabled); 160 MHz would raise CPU percentages by 1.5×.

15.6 Make the device deployable

  • Addresses: compiled-in server address and credentials; 30 s boot wait; a network change needs a rebuild (E-41).
  • Change: store Wi-Fi and server settings in NVS with a provisioning method; discover the server (for example mDNS); remove the host-wait and the M1 probes from the product build (keep them in the capture build); add TLS established once at boot if the link leaves a trusted LAN.
  • Subsystem: netstream, app_main.c, sdkconfig.product.
  • Validation: EXP-1 and EXP-2 re-run (TLS costs heap); a network-change test; a cold-boot time measurement.
  • Risks: TLS could consume the remaining RAM margin — the study estimated ≈ 16 kB steady state.

15.7 Bound the heap and automate the checks

  • Addresses: the RAM claim is measured, not enforced; parity and memory checks are run by hand.
  • Change: the study's "arena discipline" (a bounded heap region and an allocation-failure hook that reports and halts); a CI job that builds the product firmware, runs the dependency gate, checks static RAM against a ceiling, and runs the parity test on a stored recording.
  • Validation: a deliberate over-allocation must be caught.
  • Risks: none functionally; time to set up.

15.8 Compress the stream for poor networks

  • Addresses: only 180 ms of ring slack beyond the pre-roll; untested on congested Wi-Fi; L3 not computed.
  • Change: the study's IMA-ADPCM mode (4:1, negligible CPU) selectable per stream, a throughput probe, and an L3 measurement.
  • Validation: word error rate with and without ADPCM on the same recordings; overruns and L3 under a throttled link.
  • Risks: ASR accuracy loss; protocol complexity.

15.9 Improve command recognition on the server

  • Addresses: the off-the-shelf recogniser's accuracy (2/5 and 3/4 in small tests).
  • Change: a measured comparison of the open-vocabulary model, the 12-phrase grammar mode already in ingest.py, and a larger or adapted non-transformer model, on a fixed set of recorded streams (possible offline with replay_session.py).
  • Validation: word error rate and command accuracy on held-out recordings, with n reported.
  • Risks: a grammar rejects anything outside its list.

15.10 Prepare the model for commercial use

  • Addresses: two TTS voices are licensed for non-commercial use only.
  • Change: regenerate the synthetic set without en_US-l2arctic-medium and hi_IN-pratham-medium (a configuration change in gen_tts.py), retrain, and re-run the declared tests.
  • Risks: fewer accented voices in training; the new model must be re-validated on the same test sets.

16.2 How to understand this project in 15 minutes

  1. The job (2 min). Detect "Hey Vayro" on a microcontroller, then send what follows to a speech recogniser — within 256 KB RAM and 10 % idle CPU, with open-source tools and a self-trained model. Read the Executive Summary table.
  2. The data path (4 min). Look at Figure 6.1 and Figure 6.2: 160 samples every 10 ms → int16 → ring → 40 log-mel features → every 60 ms the last 100 frames → DS-CNN → average of 3 ≥ 0.80 → stream from 571 ms earlier. Everything on core 1 in one task; networking on core 0.
  3. Why you can trust the numbers (3 min). The device's features and model outputs are bit-identical to the PC's (Figure 13.1); therefore offline replay equals the device, and models can be evaluated on hours of real recordings.
  4. The results (3 min). Table 12.9: 245.0 KiB, 9.32 %/5.53 % CPU, 138 ms L2, 22/25 and 20/25 recall, 0.2 and 15.8 false triggers per hour.
  5. What went wrong and how it was fixed (3 min). One-bit I2S misalignment; maths-library rounding; IRAM code over the RAM budget; ASR blocking the network; v4 better on false triggers but worse on a new speaker → v2b shipped. The main open problem is TV/conversation false triggers.

16.3 What to study deeply

These are the concepts an evaluator is most likely to probe, in order of importance for explaining this build:

  1. Keyword spotting as a detection problem — why recall and false triggers per hour matter more than "accuracy"; exposure hours; the rule of three; why the quiet and TV numbers must be quoted together.
  2. Log-mel features and the microfrontend — window, FFT, mel filterbank, noise reduction, PCAN, log; why the filterbank stops at 6 500 Hz.
  3. Depthwise-separable CNNs and int8 quantisation — parameters vs MACs; scale and zero point; why BatchNorm disappears at export; why 66 % of the compute is in the first layer.
  4. Train/deploy parity — why "the same C code" was not enough (maths-library rounding) and how the canonical tables and hashes fix it.
  5. Memory on the ESP32-S3 — static sections, IRAM, heap, stacks, cache; how S4 is computed; why the Wi-Fi stack costs more than the model.
  6. FreeRTOS scheduling and CPU measurement — tasks, priorities, core pinning, run-time statistics and their blind spot (interrupts).
  7. I2S — bit clock, word select, slot, framing; how the alignment sweep detects a one-bit shift.
  8. The streaming protocol and pre-roll — ring indices, overrun, warm TCP, clock-offset estimation, how L2 is decomposed.
  9. Data engineering for a new keyword — TTS spelling, mixing into device-recorded beds, end-of-keyword labels, speaker-disjoint splits, hard-negative mining from live scores, declared selection rules.
  10. The trade-offs that were chosen consciously — windowed vs streaming model; smaller ring vs slack; Wi-Fi power save off; v2b over v4.
